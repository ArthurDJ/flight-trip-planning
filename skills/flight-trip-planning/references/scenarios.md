# Scenarios

How the workflow changes for trips that do not fit a simple round trip. Read only the section that applies.

## 1. One-way vs round trip

- Price both ways of buying: one round-trip booking, and two one-way bookings (same or different airlines). Show the basis on the card (`references/templates.md` → Comparison cards).
- Never assume a one-way costs half a round trip; some airlines price it that way, others charge much more.
- Two one-ways are two bookings. Cancelling one leaves the other, so flexibility can be mixed: a refundable leg for the uncertain direction and a basic fare for the certain one. Run the break-even test per leg.
- On two separate bookings, a disruption on one does not protect the other; this matters most when the two legs connect on the same day.
- If the return date is unknown, "one-way now, return later" is a real option. Compare it with a changeable round trip and say which risk each one carries.

## 2. Open-jaw and multi-city

- Search the whole trip as a multi-city itinerary on the airline site (one booking), then compare with separate one-ways.
- Use the multi-city card (`references/templates.md` → Comparison cards).
- The ground leg between open-jaw points (train, car, bus) is part of the true total: time and cost.
- Work backwards from the scarcest segment of the whole trip, not only the first one.
- A missed segment on a multi-city booking can cancel the rest of it; check the fare rules before suggesting that a segment be skipped.

## 3. Families and heavy baggage

- Bags: fee × bags × travelers × directions. Check the allowance of each fare family; child and infant allowances vary by airline.
- Seats together with children: price seat selection into the true total, or choose a fare that includes it; check the airline's family seating policy at run time.
- Use the Family / group profile: generous buffers, no tight self-transfers, no very early starts.
- Strollers and car seats: rules vary by airline; check at run time.
- Documents: each child needs their own passport for a border crossing. A child travelling with one parent may need a consent letter, depending on the countries; check at run time.

## 4. Last-minute (departure within 7 days)

- The US 24-hour rule does not apply to bookings made less than one week before departure (`references/regions.md` → Dated facts), so there may be no free-cancellation window after booking. Check the airline's own policy.
- Skip price-dynamics advice; availability comes first. Confirm on the airline site that seats exist in the selected fare.
- Documents that take time (visas, eTA / ESTA-type authorizations) may decide the trip: check them first.

## 5. Several travelers on one booking

- One booking means one fare for everyone in it. Cancelling one traveler may split the booking or change the fare; check the rules.
- If only one traveler's plans are uncertain, compare one booking with separate bookings (a refundable fare only for the uncertain traveler). Separate bookings may be handled separately in a disruption: say so.
- Documents are per traveler; different nationalities mean different entry rules.
- If travelers fly on different days, give each one a traveler summary.

## 6. Award and points options

Points optimization is out of scope. If the person already has an award option:
- Show it as a candidate with its cash taxes and fees, its change and cancel rules, and the points it costs.
- Convert points to money only with a value per point the person gives; otherwise show points and cash side by side.
- Verify award availability and rules on the airline site, like any other price.

## 7. Price tracking over time

Out of scope: tracking and alerts belong to tools built for it. What this skill adds: the cancel-and-rebook rule (`references/decision-framework.md` → Price dynamics) and the reminder in the post-booking check.
